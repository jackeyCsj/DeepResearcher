Abstract
Face recognition (FR) has emerged as a vital biometric authentication method due 
to its non-invasive nature and widespread applications in security, surveillance, 
and identity verification. However, existing FR systems face significant challenges 
in preserving user privacy, especially when deployed in cloud environments. 
Traditional approaches lack robust encryption mechanisms, making sensitive facial 
data vulnerable to breaches, adversarial attacks, and unauthorised access. This 
study introduces a novel privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR) framework that 
leverages deep learning (DL) models, specifically FaceNet and ArcFace, for extracting 
discriminative facial embeddings. The framework combines these models with 
the Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song (CKKS) homomorphic encryption scheme to enable 
secure computation on encrypted data without requiring decryption. Locality 
Preserving Projection (LPP) is applied to compress high-dimensional features while 
preserving the essential data structure, thereby enhancing performance and reducing 
processing overhead. The proposed system was evaluated on four benchmark 
datasets: CASIA3D, 105PinsFace, LFW, and Faces94. Among the models, ArcFace 
showed the best performance, achieving high accuracy and low Equal Error Rates 
(EER), with results of 99.30% accuracy on CASIA3D, 98.06% on 105PinsFace, 97.07% on 
LFW, and 99.82% on Faces94 datasets. The experimental results demonstrate that the 
proposed system is both effective and secure, making it suitable for real-world face 
recognition applications with enhanced privacy protection.

Among various biometric methods, FR stands out due to its ease of use, non-intrusive

A typical biometric system consists of two main phases: enrolment and authentica -
tion. During enrolment, biometric data is captured and processed using a feature extrac-
tor to identify key characteristics. These features are used to create a reference template, 
which is securely stored. During the authentication phase, new biometric data is cap -
tured, and a query template is generated using the same feature extractor. This template 
is then compared to the stored reference. If the templates match, access is granted; oth -
erwise, it is denied. The reliability of this process depends heavily on the quality of the 
feature extractor and the secure handling of sensitive data. As more facial recognition 
(FR) systems migrate to cloud environments, protecting facial data becomes a greater 
concern [7]. Cloud platforms offer strong computing power and storage, but they also 
create more chances for data leaks or misuse. While biometrics help reduce fraud, keep -
ing templates safe from hackers remains a significant challenge [ 8]. To tackle this issue, 
researchers have proposed various biometric template protection schemes, includ -
ing cancellable biometrics [ 9], bio-cryptosystems [ 10], and homomorphic encryption 
(HE) [11]. HE, in particular, enables computations on encrypted data without requiring 
decryption, offering strong privacy guarantees. Based on their capabilities, HE schemes 
can be classified as Partially Homomorphic Encryption (PHE) [ 12], Somewhat Homo -
morphic Encryption (SHE) [ 13], and Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE) [ 14], with 
FHE being the most powerful and suitable for secure biometric computations.
1.1 Motivation
Most privacy-preserving face recognition methods try to protect users by encrypting 
the entire high-dimensional face feature vectors produced by deep neural networks. 
However, these vectors are usually large; encrypting such large data with homomorphic 
encryption produces large ciphertexts and slows matching, which is a problem for real-
time or low-resource systems. Even though face recognition technology has improved 
significantly, many systems still do not keep data encrypted from end to end. At some 
point in the pipeline, sensitive features may be exposed in plain form, creating privacy 
risks. This shows the need for a practical system that fully protects privacy without sac -
rificing accuracy or usability. To solve this, the proposed system uses a deep-learning 
model together with the CKKS homomorphic encryption scheme. CKKS can perform 
arithmetic operations on encrypted real-valued data, making it suitable for working with 
feature vectors from CNNs. By keeping the data encrypted throughout the entire pro -
cess, the system aims to provide strong privacy protection while still maintaining reliable 
face recognition performance.

1.3 Contributions
The proposed system offers a secure and reliable face recognition system. The contribu -
tions of the proposed system are as follows:
  • A privacy-preserving face recognition framework is developed by integrating 
FaceNet/ArcFace embeddings, Locality Preserving Projection (LPP), and CKKS 
homomorphic encryption to enable encrypted feature matching without revealing 
facial information.
  • LPP is applied to deep facial embeddings to reduce feature dimensionality from 
512-D to 256-D while maintaining local geometric structure. This reduction reduces 
ciphertext size and improves the efficiency of encrypted computation with minimal 
impact on recognition accuracy.
  • CKKS encryption parameters are systematically analysed and optimised to achieve 
an effective balance between precision, security level, and computational cost.
  • The proposed framework is evaluated on four diverse datasets covering both 
controlled and uncontrolled settings, demonstrating strong adaptability and 
robustness under varying conditions.
  • A comprehensive ablation study is conducted to compare encrypted matching 
using 512-D and 256-D embeddings, showing that LPP significantly enhances 
computational efficiency while maintaining reliable recognition performance.

3 Privacy-preserving face recognition via HE and deep learning
The proposed system employs a client–server setup, as illustrated in Fig. 1, which pro -
vides an overview of the enrollment and verification stages. The system ensures secure 
face authentication using FHE while maintaining user privacy. The system has three 
main components: the client device, the Trusted Authenticator (TA), and the Cloud 
Server. During enrolment, the client device captures the user’s face image and extracts 
facial features using a Deep Convolutional Neural Network (DCNN), such as FaceNet 
or ArcFace. These models are chosen for their proven ability to produce compact, highly 
discriminative embeddings. These extracted features are then sent to the TA, who 
applies LPP to reduce dimensionality, making the features more efficient for storage and 
comparison. The reduced features, known as RLpp, are encrypted using FHE and stored 
on the Cloud Server as E(R) along with the user’s ID.

During the verification phase, the user provides a new face image, which is then pro -
cessed on the client device using the same feature extraction process. The extracted 
feature vector is sent to the TA, where LPP is applied to obtain PLpp, which is then 
encrypted to form E(P). This encrypted probe embedding is sent to the cloud server for 
matching. The Cloud Server retrieves the corresponding E(R) from storage and com -
putes the Euclidean distance between E(R) and E(P). The encrypted distance value, Dt, is 
forwarded to the TA, which decrypts it using a secret key. Finally, the system compares 
the decrypted value (D) with a predefined threshold (T) to determine whether authenti -
cation is successful. If D is below the threshold, the user is accepted; otherwise, authen -
tication is denied. This architecture ensures that face data remains protected throughout 
the process, preventing unauthorised access while maintaining efficient and accurate 
authentication.
3.1 Assumptions
In the proposed approach, the following are the assumptions:
  • The client device is considered fully trusted during both the enrollment and 
authentication phases.
  • The keys are securely stored in the trusted authenticator’s local memory throughout 
enrollment and authentication.
  • The cloud server follows an honest-but-curious model, meaning it performs the 
required computations-such as calculating the Euclidean distance-accurately but 
may attempt to learn information from the data it processes. However, the computed 
distances remain correct and reliable.

3.2 Preparation of face images for feature extraction
Before extracting facial features using deep learning models, all input images are prepro-
cessed to ensure consistency and improve recognition performance. Preprocessing helps 
the models focus on the actual facial characteristics by reducing variations caused by 
background, lighting, and pose. The first step is face detection, where the facial region is 
identified and cropped from the original image. This ensures that only the relevant part 
of the image is used for analysis. Following this, face alignment is performed using key 
facial landmarks such as the eyes and nose. Alignment standardises the face’s orienta -
tion, reducing the impact of head tilts or rotations.
Once aligned, the images are resized to match the input size expected by the models. 
For example, FaceNet requires images of size 160 × 160 pixels, while ArcFace typically 
uses 112 × 112 pixels. The final step is normalisation, where pixel values are scaled to 
the range of − 1 to 1 to minimise the impact of lighting variations and ensure consistent 
model performance across images. These preprocessing steps, illustrated in Fig. 2, are 
essential for improving the quality of the extracted features and ensuring that face recog-
nition models perform effectively.
3.3 Generation of facial embedding vectors
The proposed system leverages two well-established deep learning models, FaceNet [ 44] 
and ArcFace [45], to extract robust and discriminative facial features for face recognition 
(FR) tasks. These models are selected due to their performance on large-scale face verifi -
cation benchmarks and their learning strategies.
FaceNet [17, 44] is based on the InceptionResNetV1 architecture and was pre-trained 
on the VGGFace2 dataset. While the original FaceNet architecture was designed to 
output 128-dimensional embeddings for efficient processing, the FaceNet model used 
in the proposed system produces 512-dimensional embeddings. This expanded embed -
ding size enables the model to capture more fine-grained facial details, thereby improv -
ing recognition accuracy, especially when working with large, diverse datasets. The 
model employs triplet loss to ensure that embeddings from the same identity are closer 
together, while embeddings from different identities are pushed apart. Triplet-based

Unlike existing FaceNet-based privacy-preserving approaches that operate directly on 
raw or PCA-reduced embeddings, the proposed system introduces Locality Preserving 
Projection (LPP) on deep embeddings before encryption. This step preserves local mani -

templates and improving the efficiency of CKKS-based matching. The integration of 
FaceNet with LPP and optimised CKKS encryption has not been explored in prior work, 
representing a key novelty of the proposed framework.
ArcFace is integrated through the FaceAnalysis module from the InsightFace frame -
work. It is built on a ResNet backbone and trained using the MS-Celeb-1  M dataset. 
ArcFace outputs 512-dimensional embeddings by default and applies an additive angu -
lar margin loss to enhance intra-class compactness and inter-class separability in the 
embedding space, thereby enhancing class separation on a hypersphere. This loss for -
mulation yields highly discriminative embeddings that perform well under challenging 
conditions, such as variations in pose, illumination, and facial expression. Employing 
both models provides flexibility in embedding strategies and enables comparative per -
formance analysis across datasets and conditions.
ArcFace is used in the proposed system due to its additive angular-margin formula -
tion produces highly discriminative and well-separated embeddings, and its implemen -
tation within the InsightFace framework [ 46–48] provides stable performance on both 
balanced and imbalanced datasets. This robustness arises from ArcFace’s feature and 
weight normalisation on the hypersphere, combined with a consistent angular margin 
that reduces bias toward classes with more samples and maintains uniform decision 
boundaries across identities. Earlier margin-based approaches, such as SphereFace [ 49] 
and CosFace [ 50], also contributed to improving face recognition, but each presents 
limitations: SphereFace uses a multiplicative angular margin that can lead to optimisa -
tion instability, while CosFace applies a cosine margin in the similarity space rather than 
directly in the angular domain, resulting in less consistent geometric separation. ArcFace 
addresses these issues by enforcing a clean and geometrically intuitive additive angular 
margin, enabling more stable optimisation, stronger inter-class separability, and reliable 
embedding quality even under dimensionality reduction and encrypted matching.
3.4 Projection of high-dimensional embeddings
LPP is particularly effective for dimensionality reduction in FR systems, as it preserves 
local neighbourhood structures while reducing the dimensionality of high-dimensional 
feature embeddings from models such as FaceNet and ArcFace. In comparison, Princi -
pal Component Analysis (PCA) [ 51] captures global variance but may lose fine-grained 
local details, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) [ 52] requires class labels and priori -
tises inter-class separability, and Independent Component Analysis (ICA) [ 53] assumes 
statistical independence among features. Unlike these techniques, LPP [ 54] retains geo-
metric locality and the intrinsic manifold structure of the data, ensuring a discriminative 
and compact feature space for robust FR. By preserving intrinsic relationships within 
the data, LPP effectively handles variations in pose, lighting, and expression, enhancing 
computational efficiency while maintaining the manifold structure of facial images for

3.4.1 Rationale for using LPP
Instead of Direct 256-dimensional Embeddings, FaceNet and ArcFace are trained on 
large-scale face datasets and naturally output 512-dimensional embeddings. This repre -
sentation is well validated for generalisation across different datasets. Modifying these 
deep models to produce 256-dimensional vectors would require extensive retraining 
on millions of images, which is computationally expensive and may reduce generalisa -
tion performance. LPP provides a training-free and structure-preserving dimensionality 
reduction method. It reduces the embeddings to 256 dimensions while maintaining the 
local geometric relationships between samples. This is important for face recognition 
because the discriminative structure of the feature space must be preserved even after 
encryption. Reducing the feature dimension also has practical benefits in the encrypted

3.5 Homomorphic encryption of facial features
FHE ensures that sensitive facial information stays secure and private throughout the 
process. Even if someone accesses the data during processing, they cannot understand 
it because it remains encrypted. After dimensionality reduction using LPP , complex 
numbers are often encountered, which can be efficiently handled using CKKS FHE. This 
capability makes CKKS FHE particularly suitable for privacy-preserving FR systems, as 
it supports operations on encrypted data, including computations involving complex 
numbers. Its ability to perform encrypted computations without decryption ensures 
end-to-end security, making it an ideal choice for systems that require both high-level 
privacy and robust computational capabilities. By addressing the challenges posed by 
complex numbers and ensuring the secure handling of sensitive information, FHE sig -
nificantly enhances the reliability and security of FR systems.
3.5.1 FHE
FHE schemes enable computation on encrypted data without decryption. Several 
schemes exist, each with strengths and limitations. The Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntana -
than (BGV) scheme [ 55] and its variants like BFV (Fan–Vercauteren, an FHE scheme, 
a variant of BGV) excel at integer arithmetic but contend with noise growth. Similarly, 
the Fan–Vercauteren (FV) scheme [56] faces limitations due to noise growth. The CKKS 
scheme [57] specialises in approximate arithmetic on real and complex numbers, mak -
ing it suitable for applications like machine learning and signal processing, and particu -
larly advantageous for tasks involving complex-valued data like FR and medical image 
analysis. While efficient for approximate computations, CKKS, like the closely related 
HEAAN scheme [ 58]. The TFHE scheme [ 59] is optimised for binary operations, excel -
ling in logic-based computations but potentially limited in applicability to other data 
types. Among these, the CKKS scheme stands out for its ability to handle approximate 
arithmetic on real and complex numbers. This makes CKKS particularly well-suited for 
modern applications, such as privacy-preserving machine learning, where fixed-point 
operations are essential. CKKS introduces the concept of packed computations, enabling 
multiple plaintext values to be processed in parallel, which significantly enhances 
efficiency.

3.5.3 Justification and rationale
The CKKS homomorphic encryption scheme is used because it supports approximate 
computations over encrypted real numbers, making it suitable for encrypting facial fea -
ture data. The polynomial modulus degree n = 8192 strikes a balance between strong 
security and reasonable computation time. The modulus chain bits [60, 40, 40, 60] pro-
vide sufficient space to perform multiple encrypted operations, such as addition and 
multiplication, while keeping noise levels low. The scale factor s =2 40 helps keep pre -
cision when encoding decimal numbers, flattening the feature vectors before encryp -
tion allows efficient vector operations with CKKS. After multiplication, a relinearization 
step reduces ciphertext size and noise, improving the accuracy and speed of encrypted 
distance calculations. Finally, decryption and decoding with the secret key recover the 
approximate distance value in plaintext. These choices enable the secure computa -
tion of face recognition distances on encrypted data, without exposing sensitive face 
information.

5 Security considerations
The proposed FR system’s design aligns with ISO/IEC 24745, the global standard for pro-
tecting face information. This standard outlines the crucial needs to ensure that facial 
data is safely obtained, handled, stored, and transmitted while upholding user privacy 
and data integrity. Key aspects of compliance in the system embrace the irreversibility of 
stored templates, unlinkability, renewability, and protected processing.
Irreversibility is achieved by shrinking the original 512-dimensional features using 
LPP , followed by encryption using the CKKS FHE scheme, thereby confirming that the 
original face cannot be reconstructed. The system also facilitates unlinkability by storing 
encrypted, anonymised features unique to each application setting, effectively avoiding 
cross-matching across systems.
Renewability is enabled by the ability to regenerate templates using different projection 
matrices or encryption keys, without requiring re-enrolment of face data. All matching 
operations occur entirely in the encrypted domain, guaranteeing that raw or decrypted 
features are never exposed during handling.
Additionally, the system adheres to data minimisation and privacy-by-design princi -
ples by retaining only the most discriminating features through dimensionality reduc -
tion. These design selections collectively ensure substantial compliance with ISO/IEC 
24745, making the system secure, privacy-protecting, and well-suited for deployment in 
sensitive and security-critical environments.
6 Conclusion
The proposed system presented a privacy-preserving face authentication system that 
integrates deep learning-based feature extraction, dimensionality reduction, and homo -
morphic encryption. The system was evaluated using two models-FaceNet and ArcFace-
across multiple datasets. While both models were able to extract discriminative facial 
features, FaceNet consistently showed lower accuracy and higher error rates, particu -
larly in complex scenarios. In contrast, ArcFace delivered superior performance, making 
it more suitable for reliable face authentication tasks. To enhance privacy and reduce 
computational cost, the system employed LPP to compress the 512-dimensional feature 
vectors before encryption. LPP effectively preserved local structure while reducing fea -
ture size, allowing efficient encrypted matching without significantly affecting accuracy. 
Future work can extend this system to multi-modal biometric fusion to improve accu -
racy and security. The system performance is analysed, but lightweight optimisation for 
edge devices is limited. So Future work can focus on improving efficiency for real-time 
deployment on resource-constrained hardware. Security evaluation mainly considers 
basic attack scenarios. Future work can include testing against advanced and stronger 
security threats to further improve system robustness.
